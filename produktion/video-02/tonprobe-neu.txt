You're outside, and it's dark. Look up. Pick a star. Any one you like.

Now answer me: how far away is your star?

Nothing comes. You're not alone in that. More than three thousand people got the same question. One in four put the stars closer to us than the Sun. Closer than the Sun. Five out of six students wouldn't put a number on the nearest one at all. Of the ones who tried, one in five landed in the right range — about three people in a hundred.

So there you stand in the street with no answer. What you need is a triangle. Here is how you build yours.

Put your thumb up, arm straight out, and shut your left eye. Now swap eyes.

Your thumb jumped. The wall stayed where it was. Your two eyes sit apart, and each one looks past your thumb from its own spot. Two spots, one jump.

Now you need two spots much further apart. Stand in this street in January. Come back in July and stand on the same flagstone. You never lifted a foot, and you have moved three hundred million kilometres. Your planet carried you.

Photograph a near star from both ends of your ride, and it jumps. Same as your thumb did.

A man in Königsberg spent two years catching that jump — the same trick you just played against your wall, done properly. His telescope had its main lens sawn clean in half, one half sliding on a fine screw. Night after night he turned the screw until two images of one faint star lay exactly on top of each other. Then he read the screw off by lamplight.

That reading was the jump. Half of it, drawn as an angle, is what you were after: the parallax. Small jump, far star. That is the whole rule, and now you have it too.
